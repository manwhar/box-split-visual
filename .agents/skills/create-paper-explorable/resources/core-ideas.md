# Core Ideas

An attractive question or summary can still miss what the paper contributed. Establish the contribution from the sources, then write the actual copy and read it where readers will.

## Principles

- **Outline the research before the hook.** In a few lines: context, prior limitation, central idea, how it was realized, how it was evaluated, findings, and limits. Separate a general method from the application used to test it; an engaging question is not the contribution.
- **Let two or three ideas carry the explanation.** Prefer concrete propositions to a list of components. Each should make sense before its machinery appears.
- **Attach evidence to each idea.** Record the passage or figure, the comparison behind it, and where the claim stops. Keep the paper, its released code, later adaptations, and your teaching examples distinct. Quote only text checked against its source.
- **Write the real opening and check it in place.** Draft actual copy for the audience and read it at presentation size. Keep the main path short; derivations, parameters, and code go behind links, disclosures, or speaker notes.
- **Use interaction only where it changes understanding.** A sentence or the paper's own figure is often the clearer explanation.

## Figures

- Inspect the paper's figures, tables, and captions before designing a results view. Prefer the relevant original panel to prose or an invented chart.
- Choose by job: a method diagram explains a mechanism; a results graph supports a finding. Neither replaces the other.
- Make it readable at the real size. Enlarge one panel or reveal it in steps rather than shrinking a full page, and keep a route to the full figure. Keep the axes, units, uncertainty, baselines, and caption conditions that affect the conclusion.
- Credit the source and say what is reproduced, adapted, or redrawn. Redraw numbers only from available data or a disclosed extraction; never guess values. A static published graph needs no controls.

## Descriptions

A description (gallery card, preview, abstract) is often read without the slides. Lead with the paper's insight or new capability and why it matters, and connect the mechanism to its consequence. Keep any scope that changes the claim, such as a simulated setting or a tested population, and avoid unsupported claims of priority, universality, or human equivalence. Put the implementation, the number of demos, and the controls second, or leave them out; credits belong in their own field. A description that already conveys an insight and its significance does not need rewriting to fit a formula.

Check by reading the description alone, without the title: can a reader say what the research adds and why it matters? This applies equally to empirical, theoretical, and methods papers.

## Error codes

| Code | Symptom | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EXP-ABSTRACT-AS-INTRO | The opening compresses every method and contribution into dense prose. | Can a newcomer restate the problem and contribution after reading it? |
| EXP-CLAIM-WITHOUT-EVIDENCE | A headline has no inspectable evaluation, or implies more than its evidence shows. | Show the comparison and a sentence saying what it does not establish. |
| EXP-FIGURE-WITHOUT-CONTEXT | A figure is unreadable, cropped of its baselines or uncertainty, or redrawn with invented values. | At the real size, can a reader identify the metric, comparison, conditions, uncertainty, and source? |
| EXP-ARTIFACT-AS-CONTRIBUTION | A description lists components or things to click instead of the research idea. | Read it alone: state the contribution and its consequence, and check both against the source. |

Background: [Victor, Explorable Explanations](https://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/); [Hohman et al., Communicating with Interactive Articles](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/).
